## What is an HR annual calendar?

An HR annual calendar is a 12-month plan of your organization’s key people activities and deadlines. It helps you see what needs to happen, when preparation should start, and how each activity connects to wider business priorities.

You may also hear it referred to as an HR calendar of activities or an HR planning calendar. Whatever the name, its purpose is the same: to help you coordinate HR work with the needs of the business and the people involved.

Most HR annual calendars include activities across four areas:

- Business and workforce planning: Hiring plans, HR budgets , and reviews of the skills and roles your organization needs.

- Talent and development: Goal setting, performance reviews , pay reviews, promotions, learning programs, and succession planning .

- Employee experience: Engagement surveys , wellbeing initiatives, recognition programs, and employee communications .

- Compliance and administration: Statutory reporting, policy reviews, payroll deadlines, mandatory training, and audits.

A useful calendar also shows how these activities fit together. For example, scheduling pay reviews after performance discussions gives managers time to use review outcomes in compensation decisions. Completing workforce planning before budget discussions also helps teams make clearer hiring and resourcing requests.

Giving each quarter and month a theme takes this a step further. Instead of treating goal setting, check-ins, and reviews as separate events, a theme connects them into one story that employees and managers can follow throughout the year.

An annual HR calendar helps you stay ahead of key deadlines, recurring activities, and shifting business priorities. Having the right tools and resources makes it easier to plan earlier and keep day-to-day HR work organized.

### Q1 (January–March): Set direction with goals and strategy execution

Q1 sets the tone for the whole year. When HR helps leaders turn strategy into clear goals, roles, and hiring plans early, employees spend the rest of the year working on what matters instead of guessing. Use this quarter to agree on priorities, cascade goals, and give managers the structure they need to start strong.

### Q1 HR checklist

- Set your annual HR goals. Agree on three to five HR priorities with leadership and link each one to a business goal.

- Cascade goals to teams and individuals. Share timelines and templates so goals are in place by the end of January.

- Open priority roles. Confirm approved headcount with finance and start hiring for the roles this year’s goals depend on.

- Strengthen onboarding. Give managers a 30-60-90 day plan for every new hire.

- Clarify ownership. Update roles and responsibilities for teams that changed structure or took on new goals.

- Review Q1 goal progress. Adjust any goals that no longer fit before Q2 begins.

### January

When employees start the year without clear goals, they fill their calendars with whatever feels urgent, and by March, it’s hard to tell whether their work moved the business forward. Use January to translate company priorities into team and individual goals, so everyone knows what success looks like this year. For HR, this is also the month to set your own team’s goals and start employee productivity planning with leaders.

- [Observance] Jan. 1–31: National Mentoring Month. Launch or relaunch a mentoring program and match mentors with mentees around their new goals.

- [Holiday] Jan. 18: Martin Luther King Jr. Day (federal holiday). Plan a day of service or a learning session on inclusion.

- [Observance] Jan. 28: Data Privacy Day. Remind employees how you handle their personal data and refresh data protection training.

### February

Once goals are set, you can see which skills and roles the business needs to deliver them. February is the time to turn your hiring plan into open roles and make sure new hires become productive quickly, because the first few months shape how long people stay. Focus on a consistent interview process and an onboarding experience that runs well beyond the first week.

### March

By March, the year’s plans are in motion, and gaps in ownership start to show up as missed handoffs and duplicated work. Use this month to clarify who owns what, especially in teams that have changed structure or taken on new goals, and give managers a regular rhythm for checking in on priorities. As Q1 closes, review progress on January’s goals and adjust any that no longer fit.

- [Observance] Mar. 20: International Day of Happiness. Use it as a prompt to ask teams what makes their working day better.

### Q2 (April–June): Grow and check in through wellbeing, learning, and feedback

Q2 is when the year’s plans turn into day-to-day work, and when early signs of pressure start to show. Use this quarter to check how people are coping, invest in the skills they need, and hold honest mid-year conversations about progress. Feedback quality matters here, too. Gallup data shows that 80% of employees who report receiving meaningful feedback in the past week are fully engaged.

### Q2 HR checklist

- Check in on workload and wellbeing. Run a short pulse survey and act on what teams report before mid-year.

- Identify skill gaps. Compare current skills with what this year’s goals require and update your L&D plan.

- Prepare managers for mid-year reviews. Refresh templates, run short training on feedback conversations, and set a completion date in late June.

- Turn skill gaps into development plans. Help employees agree on individual learning goals with their managers.

- Analyze Q1 engagement and attrition data. Look for patterns by team or manager before mid-year reviews begin.

- Plan summer coverage. Confirm leave schedules and backfill arrangements for peak vacation months.

### April

When workloads stay high for months, productivity drops and burnout follows, often in the teams delivering your most important goals. April is a good month to check how employees are coping, because you still have time to adjust priorities before mid-year. Ask employees about workload and support, then work with managers to pause or remove low-value work.

- [Observance] Apr. 21: Administrative Professionals Day. Recognize administrative professionals and check they have the tools and development opportunities they need.

### May

As the business changes, the skills employees needed in January may not be the ones they need by the end of the year. Use May to identify skill gaps against your goals and turn them into targeted learning plans, so your L&D budget goes where it will have the most impact. Involve managers early, since they see skill gaps in daily work before they show up in performance data.

### June

Mid-year is your chance to course-correct while there’s still time to change the outcome. Instead of waiting for the year-end review, use June to help managers hold short, honest conversations about progress, priorities, and the support employees need. Revisit the goals employees set in January and update the ones that no longer reflect the business.

### Q3 (July–September): Retain and plan for the future workforce

Q3 is the planning quarter for the year ahead. With half-year data in hand, you can see who is engaged, who may be thinking of leaving, and which roles the business will need next. Use this quarter to act on engagement findings, plan your future workforce, and give employees a clear view of where their careers can go, so you go into budget season with evidence rather than assumptions.

### Q3 HR checklist

- Act on engagement findings. Share results with employees and commit to two or three visible actions.

- Hold stay interviews. Prioritize high performers and employees in critical roles.

- Build next year’s workforce plan. Map headcount and skills against future business needs before budget discussions.

- Review succession for critical roles. Use performance and potential data to identify and develop successors.

- Publish career paths. Show employees the skills and steps they need to move up or across.

- Start benefits and compensation planning. Agree on renewal timelines and merit, promotion, and bonus assumptions with finance.

### July

Summer is a common time for employees to rethink their jobs, especially after mid-year conversations. Use July to understand what keeps your people engaged and act visibly on what you hear, because employees stop answering surveys when nothing changes. Pay close attention to high performers and critical roles, where an unexpected resignation hurts most.

### August

Before budgets are set for next year, you need a clear picture of the roles, skills, and headcount the business will need. Use August to compare your current workforce with future needs and identify the critical roles that would hurt most if they were left empty. When you complete this work before budget discussions, HR walks in with a plan instead of reacting to requests.

Use September to show people where their careers can go through clear career paths, development plans, and regular coaching from their managers. ### Q4 (October–December): Review, reward, and plan the year ahead

Q4 combines closing out the current year with planning the next. Year-end reviews, compensation decisions, open enrollment, and next year’s HR plan all compete for attention in a short period, so sequence them carefully. As you build next year’s plan, include a clear position on HR technology and AI. Gartner advises HR leaders to develop an HR-specific AI strategy that aligns with broader business goals.

### Q4 HR checklist

- Build next year’s HR plan and budget. Link each initiative to a business goal and align headcount, programs, and technology investment with finance.

- Complete year-end reviews and calibration. Hold calibration sessions before compensation decisions are locked.

- Finalize compensation decisions. Check for unexplained pay gaps, then approve merit increases, promotions, and bonuses.

- Run open enrollment. Track completion weekly and follow up with employees who haven’t made elections.

- Recognize the year’s contributions. Help managers thank employees for specific work and announce promotions.

- Get processes ready for January. Update HR SOPs, publish the holiday schedule, and confirm coverage for critical roles.

## How to create an HR annual calendar

Use the themed calendar above as a starting point, then tailor it to your organization’s fiscal year, workforce footprint, compliance needs, and business peaks. Follow these six steps to build your own HR annual calendar.

### Map your business cycle

Start with your organization’s operating rhythm: fiscal year, budget deadlines, board meetings, peak trading periods, and planned system launches. These fixed points shape when HR activities can happen.

For example, if compensation budgets are due to finance in October, you may need performance data by September. Deloitte research found that 7 in 10 business leaders see speed and agility as their primary competitive strategy, so flag business dates that may shift.

### Add compliance deadlines first

Compliance obligations are date-specific and often non-negotiable, so add them before anything else. Our free Excel template includes key U.S. federal deadlines, such as payroll tax filings, W-2s, ACA reporting, Form 5500, and EEO-1. Add the state and local requirements, required postings, and benefits notices that apply where your employees work. Then set internal due dates two to four weeks before each statutory deadline.

### List recurring HR activities by category

List every recurring people process, including performance reviews, merit cycles, open enrollment, surveys, talent reviews , and mandatory training.

For each activity, note how long it takes, who is involved, and what it depends on. This can help you spot activities that continue out of habit rather than clear business need.

### Assign owners and lead times

Give each activity one accountable owner and a preparation start date. Typical lead times include:

- Open enrollment: 90–120 days

- Compensation cycles: 8–12 weeks

- Engagement surveys: 6–8 weeks

- Performance reviews: 4–6 weeks

Build in manager capacity, too. Gartner’s research found that 47% of managers say more is expected of them than a year ago, and only 41% of employees say their manager helps them prioritize their work.

### Flag dependencies and conflicts

Map how activities depend on each other. Performance ratings may feed merit decisions, talent reviews may inform succession plans, and engagement results may shape action plans.

Before publishing the calendar, check for activities that run in the wrong order, overlap with business peaks, or create too much pressure for the same teams.

### Share it and review it quarterly

Publish the calendar where managers already work, such as a shared calendar, HRIS , or planning tool. Walk managers through the periods that need their input, clarify ownership, and review the calendar each quarter against business conditions. Note what ran late, what clashed, and what no longer adds enough value, then rebuild the calendar annually alongside budget planning.
